Tense traps

Choose the only option that is correct and natural. Read the whole sentence first: the time words decide.

  1. I ___ her since we were six.
    have known: since + a state that continues until now. Know is a state verb, so no continuous form.
  2. When I got to the cinema, the film ___, so I missed the beginning.
    had already started: the film started before another past moment (when I got there) → past perfect.
  3. I ___ my keys yesterday and I still can't find them.
    lost: yesterday is a finished time → past simple, even though the result is still true.
  4. This is the first time I ___ sushi.
    have eaten: after It's / This is the first time… English always uses the present perfect (German uses the present tense here, which is the trap).
  5. By the time you ___ this letter, I will have left.
    read: after time conjunctions (when, by the time, as soon as, until, before) we use a present form, never will.
  6. Look at those black clouds! It ___ rain.
    is going to: a prediction based on evidence you can see right now.
  7. "Why are your hands so dirty?" – "I ___ in the garden."
    have been working: an activity that has just stopped and left a visible result (dirty hands) → present perfect continuous.
  8. She ___ three novels so far, and she's working on a fourth.
    has written: when we count finished results (three novels), we use the simple form. The continuous form focuses on the activity, not the number.
  9. This time next week we ___ on a beach in Crete.
    will be lying: an action in progress at a specific future moment → future continuous.
  10. He was exhausted because he ___ for twelve hours.
    had been driving: a long activity up to a past moment that explains a past result → past perfect continuous.
  11. Hurry up! According to the timetable, the bus ___ at 7:42.
    leaves: timetables and fixed schedules use the present simple, even for the future.
  12. I ___ that you were coming. Nobody told me!
    didn't know: the situation is over (now I know), and know has no continuous form.
  13. We ___ for an hour when the guide finally showed up.
    had been waiting: duration (for an hour) up to a past moment (when the guide showed up).
  14. Don't phone at eight; we ___ dinner then.
    will be having: in the middle of an activity at a future time. (Have dinner is an action, so continuous is fine.)
  15. By 2035, scientists ___ a cure, I hope.
    will have found: by + future time → completed before that point → future perfect.
  16. I ___ to call you, but I completely forgot.
    was going to: a plan in the past that did not happen ("future in the past").
  17. The soup ___ delicious. Can I have some more?
    tastes: taste as a quality (= has a flavour) is a state verb. Compare: The chef is tasting the soup = action, he is trying it.
  18. "You look worried." – "I ___ about the exam tomorrow."
    I'm thinking: think about = a mental activity happening now → continuous. I think (that)… = opinion → simple.

Error hunt

Eleven sentences contain one mistake; four are correct. If a sentence is correct, type correct. Otherwise type only the corrected words (for example: have lived).

  1. I live in Graz since 2019.
    [[~have lived|have been living]]
    since → present perfect (simple or continuous).
  2. She told me she has finished the project the day before.
    [[~had finished]]
    Reported speech + the day before → past perfect.
  3. If it will rain tomorrow, we'll stay inside.
    [[~if it rains|rains]]
    First conditional: present simple in the if-clause.
  4. We've been knowing each other for ages.
    [[~have known|we have known]]
    Know is a state verb: no continuous form.
  5. When did you last see your grandparents?
    [[correct]]
    Correct: last points to a finished moment in the past → past simple.
  6. I'm tired because I'm working since seven o'clock.
    [[~have been working|i have been working]]
    Duration up to now → present perfect continuous.
  7. Hardly had I sat down when the fire alarm went off.
    [[correct]]
    Correct: inversion after Hardly … when. You will practise this in Part 12.
  8. The police is investigating the case.
    [[~are investigating|police are|are]]
    Police is always plural in English.
  9. I didn't see him since last summer.
    [[~haven't seen|have not seen]]
    since → present perfect.
  10. This time tomorrow I'll fly to New York.
    [[~will be flying]]
    In progress at a future moment → future continuous.
  11. He's used to get up early.
    [[~used to getting up|getting up|getting]]
    be used to + -ing (= accustomed to). Compare: He used to get up early = he did it in the past.
  12. It's high time we left.
    [[correct]]
    Correct: It's (high) time + past simple, with present meaning.
  13. I wish I didn't say that to her yesterday.
    [[~hadn't said|had not said]]
    Regret about the past → wish + past perfect.
  14. The number of students have increased this year.
    [[~has increased|has]]
    The number of takes a singular verb. (But: A number of students have = many students.)
  15. Neither of my brothers likes football.
    [[correct]]
    Correct: neither of normally takes a singular verb in formal English.

Reading: unfinished business

Read the article carefully (about 6 minutes). Then do sections A to D. Paragraph numbers are on the left.

The Unfinished Business of the Brain

1

In the 1920s, a young Lithuanian-born psychologist named Bluma Zeigarnik was sitting in a Berlin café with her supervisor, Kurt Lewin, when something odd caught their attention. The waiter, who had taken a large group's order without writing anything down, could recall every dish perfectly, until the bill had been paid. Asked about the order a few minutes later, he could barely remember a thing. The task was complete, and his memory, it seemed, had simply let it go.

2

Intrigued, Zeigarnik designed a series of experiments back at the laboratory. Participants were given simple tasks such as solving puzzles, threading beads and making clay figures, and were interrupted before they could finish some of them. Afterwards, when asked to list the tasks they had worked on, they remembered the interrupted ones roughly twice as often as the completed ones. The phenomenon became known as the Zeigarnik effect: an unfinished task appears to keep a small part of our mind busy until we return to it.

3

Writers and television producers figured this out long before psychologists gave it a name. Charles Dickens published many of his novels in monthly instalments, and readers waited impatiently for the next part. Today's streaming services end episodes at the most painful possible moment for exactly the same reason, and the "next episode" button that appears within seconds is no accident.

4

Yet the story is not quite as neat as it is often told. Attempts to repeat Zeigarnik's results have produced mixed findings, and some researchers argue that the effect depends heavily on how motivated people are to finish the task in the first place. A tedious chore that was interrupted may be forgotten with relief rather than remembered with longing.

5

What does seem clear is that unfinished tasks can weigh on us. Studies in the 2010s suggested that simply making a concrete plan for when and how to complete a task can reduce the mental "noise" it creates, even if the task itself remains undone. The brain, in other words, may not need us to finish everything; it may simply need reassurance that we will.

6

So the next time you cannot stop thinking about the essay you have not started, you could try writing down exactly when you will start it. It might not make the essay any easier, but it could give your mind permission to think about something else.

A · Multiple choice

  1. What surprised Zeigarnik and Lewin about the waiter?
    B. The surprise is the sudden forgetting after payment (para 1). A is true but it is not what was "odd".
  2. According to paragraph 2, the participants …
    B. "remembered the interrupted ones roughly twice as often". D is too extreme: they remembered fewer, not none.
  3. The writer mentions Charles Dickens in order to show that …
    B. "figured this out long before psychologists gave it a name".
  4. What is the main purpose of paragraph 4?
    B. "not quite as neat as it is often told" = the writer limits the claim without rejecting it. A is too strong.
  5. The tone of the final paragraph is best described as …
    A. Direct address ("you could try"), modest promises ("might", "could").

B · True, False or Not Given?

True = the text says so. False = the text says the opposite. Not given = the text does not say. This is the hardest distinction in reading exams.

  1. Zeigarnik was born in Germany.
    False: she was "Lithuanian-born".
  2. The waiter wrote the order down.
    False: "without writing anything down".
  3. All of Zeigarnik's participants were university students.
    Not given: the text says nothing about who the participants were.
  4. Some researchers believe motivation plays an important role in the effect.
    True: "depends heavily on how motivated people are".
  5. Streaming services employ psychologists to design their cliffhangers.
    Not given: the text says the cliffhangers are deliberate, but not who designs them. A classic trap.
  6. Every later study confirmed Zeigarnik's original results.
    False: "mixed findings".
  7. Making a plan can reduce how much an unfinished task occupies your thoughts.
    True: para 5.

D · Think further

  1. Explain in your own words what the writer means by: "it may simply need reassurance that we will."

    The writer suggests that our brain does not insist on finishing every task immediately. What it wants is a sign that the task is under control: once we have decided when and how we will do it, the brain can stop reminding us and relax.

  2. Why is the "next episode" button described as "no accident"? (1–2 sentences)

    It is placed there on purpose: the episode ends on an unresolved moment, so viewers feel the urge to continue, and the button makes it effortless to do so before that tension fades.

  3. Could the Zeigarnik effect help you study? Give one concrete idea (50–80 words).

    One idea is to stop a study session in the middle of an exercise rather than at a natural break. Because the task is unfinished, my mind is likely to return to it, so it is easier to start again the next day. In addition, writing down exactly when I will continue could prevent the unfinished task from making me anxious while I do something else.

Key word transformations

Complete the second sentence so that it means the same as the first. Use the key word without changing it. Write three to six words, including the key word. This is a real C1 (Cambridge Advanced) task type.

  1. I started learning the piano three years ago.
    BEEN
    I [[have been learning|have been playing]] the piano for three years.
  2. It's ages since I last saw a good film.
    SEEN
    I [[haven't seen|have not seen]] a good film for ages.
  3. The concert started before we arrived.
    ALREADY
    When we arrived, the concert [[had already started|had already begun]].
  4. "I'll call you tomorrow," he said to me.
    WOULD
    He told me [[he would call me|that he would call me|he would phone me|he would ring me|that he would phone me]] the next day.
  5. I regret not studying harder for the test.
    WISH
    I [[wish i had studied|wish i'd studied]] harder for the test.
  6. We didn't go out because it was raining.
    IF
    [[if it had not been|if it hadn't been]] raining, we would have gone out.
  7. I've never seen such a beautiful sunset.
    EVER
    It's the most beautiful sunset [[i have ever|i've ever]] seen.
  8. As soon as I closed the door, the phone rang.
    SOONER
    No [[sooner had i closed]] the door than the phone rang.
    Inversion: No sooner had I … than …
  9. People say that he is a brilliant chess player.
    SAID
    He [[is said to be]] a brilliant chess player.
    Passive reporting structure: He is said / believed / thought to be …
  10. They are building a new library in our town.
    BUILT
    A new library [[is being built]] in our town.
  11. It wasn't necessary for you to bring a present, but you did.
    NEED
    You [[needn't have brought|need not have brought]] a present.
    needn't have done = you did it, but it wasn't necessary. (didn't need to bring would usually mean you didn't bring one.)
  12. I'm sure she didn't take your phone.
    CAN'T
    She [[can't have taken|cannot have taken|can not have taken]] your phone.
    Deduction about the past: can't have / must have / might have + past participle.
  13. It's possible that they missed the train.
    MAY
    They [[may have missed]] the train.
  14. I haven't been to the cinema for six months.
    TIME
    The [[last time i went to|last time i was at]] the cinema was six months ago.
  15. "Don't touch the paintings," the guide told us.
    NOT
    The guide told us [[not to touch]] the paintings.
  16. Please don't smoke in here.
    RATHER
    I'd [[rather you didn't|rather you did not]] smoke in here.
    I'd rather + someone + past simple when we talk about another person's action.

Accents and identity

For a long time it was considered almost [[impossible]] (POSSIBLE) for adults to lose their foreign accent. Recent research, however, paints a far more [[nuanced]] (NUANCE) picture. The [[ability]] (ABLE) to imitate new sounds accurately varies [[considerably]] (CONSIDER) from person to person, and it seems to depend less on age than on [[motivation]] (MOTIVATE) and the amount of [[exposure]] (EXPOSE) to the language.

Interestingly, many learners feel a certain [[reluctance]] (RELUCTANT) to sound "too native", as if adopting a new accent were an act of [[disloyalty]] (LOYAL) towards their own culture. Linguists stress that an accent is in no way a sign of [[incompetence]] (COMPETENT); many highly [[successful]] (SUCCEED) speakers of English have strong accents. What [[arguably]] (ARGUE) matters most is [[comprehensibility]] (COMPREHEND): being easy to understand. Sounding like a newsreader is a nice bonus, but hardly a [[requirement]] (REQUIRE).

The earl who couldn't leave the table

Few foods are [[as]] widely eaten as the sandwich, yet its origins are surprisingly uncertain. According to the most popular story, John Montagu, the fourth Earl of Sandwich, was [[so]] fond of card games that he refused to leave the table for meals. Instead, he asked for meat to be served between two slices of bread, [[so]] that he could keep one hand free for his cards.

[[Whether]] the story is true or not, historians cannot say for certain. People had [[been]] eating bread with fillings for centuries before the Earl was born, [[but|yet]] it was his name that stuck. Some biographers even claim that he was more likely to have eaten his sandwiches at his desk, [[where|as|since|because]] he worked long hours for the government.

[[No]] matter which version you believe, one thing is clear: the sandwich owes its name, if not its existence, [[to]] an eighteenth-century aristocrat. Literary analysis: The last bus

Read this short story extract twice: once for the plot, once for how it is written. Then answer the questions in full sentences. The model answers are suggestions; good answers can look different.

The Last Bus

1

The 23 was late again. Nora stood under the broken shelter, watching the rain slide off the plastic roof in thin silver threads, and tried not to look at the boy at the other end of the bench.

2

She had known Daniel since primary school. They had built a dam in the stream behind his house, had invented a language that nobody else could understand, had promised, with the seriousness only ten-year-olds can manage, that they would never, ever stop being friends. Then, last spring, something had shifted. She still couldn't say exactly what. There had been no argument, no slammed door. He had simply started sitting somewhere else.

3

Now he was scrolling through his phone as if it contained the answer to a question she hadn't asked.

4

"It's always late on Thursdays," he said suddenly, without looking up.

5

Nora was so surprised that she laughed, a short, startled sound that the rain swallowed almost at once. "I know," she said. "I've been catching it for three years."

6

"Me too."

7

They both knew that. For a moment neither of them said anything. A car hissed past, its headlights turning the puddles gold and then black again.

8

"Do you still remember any of it?" he asked. "The language?"

9

She did. She remembered all of it. "Not really," she said.

10

The bus came round the corner then, enormous and bright, and its doors opened with a sigh. Daniel stood up first. On the step he hesitated, turned, and said one word she had not heard anyone say in four years.

11

Then he climbed on and walked to the back, and Nora stood in the rain a second longer than she needed to, holding the word in her mouth like something warm, before she followed him.

  1. Who tells the story, and from whose point of view do we see the events?
    C. "she / her", and we only learn Nora's thoughts (she remembered all of it). Daniel's feelings stay a mystery, which creates tension.
  2. Paragraph 2 uses the past perfect seven times. Why does the writer switch to this tense here?

    The past perfect marks a flashback: it takes us back to events that happened before the scene at the bus stop. It gives us the background of the friendship in a compressed way, and the repetition ("had built … had invented … had promised") creates a rhythm that suggests how much they shared, which makes the loss in "had shifted" feel stronger.

  3. "There had been no argument, no slammed door." What does this sentence tell us about how the friendship ended?

    It ended quietly, without a clear reason or a dramatic moment. This is what makes it painful and confusing for Nora: there is nothing she can point to or apologise for, so she "still couldn't say exactly what" happened.

  4. "…as if it contained the answer to a question she hadn't asked." Explain the effect of this simile.

    The simile suggests that Daniel is using his phone as a shield to avoid her. It also hints that there are unspoken questions between them, above all why he stopped being her friend. Nora has not dared to ask, and the phone seems to stand in for an answer she cannot get.

  5. Why does Nora answer "Not really" although she remembers all of the language?

    She protects herself. She does not know why Daniel is asking, and admitting that she remembers everything would show how much the friendship still means to her. If he only meant it as small talk, she would feel exposed, so she pretends not to care.

  6. Find two images of light, sound or weather and explain how they reflect the mood.

    The rain "swallowed" her laugh: a moment of warmth disappears at once, just as their conversation keeps fading into silence. The headlights turn the puddles "gold and then black again", which mirrors brief moments of hope followed by awkwardness. Finally the bus arrives "enormous and bright" and its doors open "with a sigh", a sound of relief that matches the release of tension at the end.

  7. "They both knew that." What is the effect of this very short sentence?

    It points out the awkwardness of the conversation: they are stating facts both already know because neither can say what really matters. Its shortness creates a pause and a slightly ironic tone.

  8. What is the significance of the "one word"? And what does "like something warm" suggest?

    The word comes from their secret childhood language. By saying it, Daniel shows that he has not forgotten either, and it works as an offer to reconnect. "Like something warm" suggests comfort and hope after the cold, wet scene: Nora holds on to the moment because it means the friendship might not be lost.

  9. The ending is open. Write the next 4–6 sentences in the same style (past tenses, third person, Nora's perspective).

    The back seat was damp and smelled of wet coats. Daniel had put his phone away; his hands were resting on his knees as if he did not know what to do with them. Nora sat down next to him, leaving exactly enough space for a schoolbag between them. For two stops neither of them spoke. Then, very quietly, she answered him in the same language, and out of the corner of her eye she saw him smile at the window.

Reported speech and reporting verbs

Report the sentences. Shift the tenses back where necessary. In 9–12, the reporting verb needs a special pattern (infinitive or -ing).

  1. "I'm exhausted." → She said she [[was]] exhausted.
  2. "I've lost my wallet." → He said he [[had lost]] his wallet.
  3. "We'll meet you at the station." → They said they [[would meet]] us at the station.
  4. "I was watching TV when you called." → She said she [[had been watching|was watching]] TV when I [[had called|called]].
    Strict backshift: had been watching … had called. In spoken English the past continuous / past simple are often kept.
  5. "Are you coming to the party?" → She asked me [[if i was coming|whether i was coming]] to the party.
    Reported yes/no question: if / whether + normal word order (no inversion!).
  6. "Where did you buy that jacket?" → He asked me where [[i had bought]] that jacket.
  7. "Don't be late!" → Mum told me [[not to be]] late.
  8. "I can't swim." → He admitted that he [[couldn't|could not]] swim.
  9. "You should apologise." → She advised me [[to apologise|to apologize]].
  10. "I didn't break the vase!" → He denied [[breaking|having broken]] the vase.
    deny + -ing (never deny to break).
  11. "Let's go to the cinema." → She suggested [[going|that we go|that we should go|we go]] to the cinema.
    suggest + -ing or suggest that we (should) go. Never suggested us to go.
  12. "I'm sorry I forgot your birthday." → He apologised for [[forgetting|having forgotten]] my birthday.

Conditionals, wishes, unreal past

Put the verbs into the correct form. Several are mixed conditionals (past cause → present result, or present cause → past result).

  1. If I [[had known]] (know) you were in hospital, I would have visited you.
  2. If she [[hadn't missed|had not missed]] (not / miss) the bus this morning, she would be here now.
    Mixed: past condition → present result.
  3. If I [[were|was]] (be) you, I'd accept the offer.
  4. Unless you [[hurry]] (hurry), you'll miss the start.
    unless = if … not. Present simple; no extra negation.
  5. I wish it [[would stop]] (stop) raining. It's been raining all week!
    wish … would = irritation, a wish for someone/something else to change.
  6. I wish I [[could]] (can) speak Japanese.
  7. If only I [[hadn't said|had not said]] (not / say) that to her yesterday.
  8. [[had]] (have) I known about the problem, I would have helped.
    Formal inversion: Had I known = If I had known.
  9. If he were more organised, he [[wouldn't have lost|would not have lost]] (not / lose) his homework last week.
    Mixed: present characteristic → past result.
  10. Provided that everyone [[agrees]] (agree), we'll start tomorrow.
  11. It's time you [[started]] (start) revising for your exams.
  12. Should you [[need]] (need) any help, just let me know.
    Should you need = If you happen to need (formal).
  13. I'd rather you [[didn't tell|did not tell]] (not / tell) anyone about this.
  14. If it [[hadn't been|had not been]] (not / be) for your help, I would never have finished.
  15. Suppose you [[won]] (win) a million euros, what would you do first?

Inversion and emphasis

Rewrite the sentences starting with the words given. After negative or restrictive words at the start, English uses question word order (Never have I seen…). This is typical of formal, written C1 English.

  1. I have never seen such chaos.
    Never [[have i seen]] such chaos.
  2. She didn't realise until later that she had lost her passport.
    Not until later [[did she realise|did she realize]] that she had lost her passport.
  3. We had hardly left the house when it started to snow.
    Hardly [[had we left]] the house when it started to snow.
  4. You must not open this door under any circumstances.
    Under no circumstances [[must you open|should you open]] this door.
  5. He sings and he also plays the guitar.
    Not only [[does he sing]], but he also plays the guitar.
  6. I only understood the joke when she explained it.
    Only when she explained it [[did i understand|did i get]] the joke.
  7. I rarely go to the cinema these days.
    Rarely [[do i go]] to the cinema these days.
  8. She had no idea what was about to happen.
    Little [[did she know]] what was about to happen.
  9. I need a long holiday.
    What [[i need is]] a long holiday.

Task A · Essay (220–260 words)

Your class has had a discussion about where teenagers learn the most important things in life. You have made the notes below.

  • school
  • the internet
  • family

Some opinions expressed in the discussion:

  • "School teaches you how to think."
  • "You can learn anything online in five minutes."
  • "Parents teach you things no teacher can."

Write an essay discussing two of the places in your notes. Explain which place teaches teenagers more, giving reasons. You may use the opinions, but use your own words as far as possible.

OpeningIt is often claimed that … / There is an ongoing debate about …
ContrastWhile this is true to some extent, … / On the other hand, … / Admittedly, …
AddingWhat is more, … / Not only … but also … / In addition, …
ConcludingOn balance, … / All things considered, … / I would therefore argue that …

Checklist: clear title · introduction that states the question · one paragraph per place · at least one counter-argument · conclusion with a clear opinion · formal style (no contractions, no "stuff", "a lot of") · at least two advanced structures (inversion, cleft sentence, mixed conditional).

Where do teenagers really learn what matters?

It is often claimed that young people today learn more from their screens than from their teachers. While there is some truth in this, I would argue that school and the internet each have a role that the other cannot fill.

The greatest strength of the internet is undoubtedly its speed and range. Within seconds, a curious teenager can watch a lecture by a Nobel Prize winner, learn the basics of a new language or find out how to repair a bicycle. However, this abundance comes at a price. Online information is rarely checked, and algorithms tend to show us what we already agree with. Without the ability to judge sources critically, a young person may end up knowing a great deal of trivia while understanding very little.

This is precisely where school comes in. Although lessons can feel slow and repetitive, school is one of the few places where teenagers are required to engage with ideas they did not choose themselves, to justify their opinions and to work with people they might never otherwise meet. Alongside facts, it teaches the habits of thinking that make online learning useful in the first place.

On balance, I believe school still has the edge, because it teaches young people what to do with information once they have found it. Ideally, of course, the two would work together: a curious mind that explores the internet freely, guided by a critical mind that school has helped to train.

Task B · Story (180–220 words)

Begin your story with this sentence: "I had been waiting for this moment for months, but when it finally came, I wasn't ready."

Use at least: past simple · past continuous · past perfect simple · past perfect continuous · one would for "future in the past" · two different sentence starters (Suddenly, Moments later, Without warning…) · one piece of direct speech.

I had been waiting for this moment for months, but when it finally came, I wasn't ready. The lights in the hall had dimmed, the audience was still whispering, and somewhere behind the curtain someone was tuning a violin for the hundredth time.

I had practised my speech every evening since September. I had recorded it on my phone, performed it for the cat and even recited it in the shower. Now, standing in the wings, I could not remember the first sentence.

"You'll be fine," whispered the organiser, who had been running around all week and looked even more nervous than I did. I nodded, although I was quite sure I wouldn't be.

Then my name was called. I walked to the microphone, looked out at the rows of faces and opened my mouth. Nothing came out. For three endless seconds the hall was completely silent.

Suddenly, I noticed my little brother in the front row. He was holding up a sheet of paper on which he had written, in enormous letters, the first word of my speech.

I laughed, and the rest came back to me at once. Later that evening I would learn that I had won second prize, but that was not the moment I would remember.
