Die Strategie zur Eins

Die rezeptiven Teile sind deine Punktebank. In Reading, Listening und Language in Use gibt es eindeutige Lösungen – wer die Formate beherrscht, holt hier verlässlich über 90 %.

Writing entscheidet über die Eins. Wer alle Inhaltspunkte ausführlich bearbeitet und das Layout der Textsorte trifft, sichert sich das wichtigste Kriterium. Danach zählt sprachliche Vielfalt: Konditionalsätze, Passiv, Relativsätze, präzise Verben.

Wortgrenzen und Wortanzahl sind heilig. Bei Kurzantworten kosten fünf Wörter den Punkt, beim Schreiben weicht man am besten nur wenig von der angegebenen Wortanzahl ab.

R

Reading – Strategien für jedes Format

Beim Leseverstehen geht es selten darum, jedes Wort zu kennen. Entscheidend ist, die Aufgabe als Suchauftrag zu verstehen: Du weißt, was du suchst, und findest die Stelle, die es belegt.

True/False mit Begründung

Die Aussagen folgen der Reihenfolge des Textes. Unterstreiche das Schlüsselwort der Aussage und suche im Text nach Synonymen oder Umschreibungen. Als Begründung schreibst du die ersten vier Wörter des Satzes, der die Antwort belegt.

Punkt nur, wenn True/False und Begründung stimmen.

Multiple Choice

Lies zuerst nur die Frage, nicht die Optionen, und suche die Stelle im Text. Formuliere selbst eine Antwort und vergleiche erst dann mit A–D. Falsche Optionen enthalten oft Wörter aus dem Text, sagen aber etwas anderes.

Zuordnen (fehlende Sätze)

Achte auf Bindeglieder: Pronomen (this, they, it), Verbindungswörter (however, instead), Zeitangaben und Wiederholungen. Der fehlende Satz muss nach vorne und nach hinten passen. Zwei Optionen bleiben übrig.

Kurzantworten

Maximal vier Wörter – Zahlen zählen als Wörter. Ganze Sätze sind unnötig. Die Antwort muss inhaltlich stimmen, kleine Rechtschreibfehler sind meist kein Problem, solange das Wort eindeutig erkennbar bleibt.

Schritt für Schritt

True/False mit Begründung lösen

Text: The museum, which opened in 1998, attracts around 200,000 visitors a year. Most of them come for the dinosaur skeletons. Surprisingly, the gift shop makes more money than the ticket office.

Aussage: The museum earns most of its money from entrance fees.
1
Schlüsselbegriff markieren: earns most of its money und entrance fees.
2
Synonyme im Text suchen: makes more money ≈ earns money, ticket office ≈ entrance fees.
3
Vergleichen: Der Shop verdient mehr als die Kassa – also stammt das meiste Geld nicht aus Eintrittsgebühren.
✓

L

Listening – vorbereitet zuhören

Beim Hörverstehen gewinnst du die meisten Punkte, bevor der Text überhaupt beginnt: in der Vorbereitungszeit.

Vor dem Hören

Lies alle Fragen und markiere Schlüsselwörter. Überlege, welche Art von Information gesucht ist: eine Zahl, ein Ort, ein Grund, eine Person? Dann hörst du gezielt.

Erster Durchgang

Notiere Stichworte und kreuze vorläufig an. Hänge nicht an einer verpassten Frage fest – der Text läuft weiter, und du hörst ihn ein zweites Mal.

Zweiter Durchgang

Konzentriere dich auf die offenen und unsicheren Fragen. Achte auf Korrekturen der Sprecher: „at four – no, sorry, at five“. Die zweite Angabe zählt.

Distraktoren erkennen

Oft werden mehrere Optionen erwähnt, aber nur eine passt zur Frage. Typisch: eine Zahl, die genannt und dann geändert wird, oder etwas, das jemand nicht getan hat.

Tipp für das Training: In der Simulation liest der Browser die Hörtexte mit einer englischen Stimme vor. Höre wie in der Matura genau zweimal – das trainiert die Konzentration. Die Transkripte erscheinen erst nach der Abgabe.

Grammatik Passiv, Relativsätze, Reported Speech

Passive: be + past participle – The bridge was built in 1890. · The results will be published tomorrow.

Relative clauses: who (Personen), which (Dinge), that (beides, nur in notwendigen Relativsätzen), whose (Besitz), where (Ort). Bei Zusatzinformation stehen Kommas, und that ist dort nicht möglich: My sister, who lives in Graz, is a nurse.

Reported speech: Zeit rückt meist einen Schritt zurück: „I am tired“ → She said (that) she was tired.  say ohne Person, tell mit Person: She told me that …

Schritt für Schritt

Einen Essay in fünf Schritten planen

Aufgabe: Write an essay on whether schools should replace printed textbooks with tablets. Discuss advantages, point out disadvantages and give your opinion. (ca. 400 Wörter)
1
Inhaltspunkte zählen und abhaken: 1. advantages, 2. disadvantages, 3. opinion. Jeder Punkt bekommt mindestens einen eigenen Absatz.
2
Ideen sammeln (5 Minuten): Vorteile – leichtere Schultasche, interaktive Inhalte, aktuell. Nachteile – Ablenkung, Kosten, Bildschirmzeit.
3
Gliederung: Einleitung (≈ 60 Wörter) · Vorteile (≈ 100) · Nachteile (≈ 100) · eigene Meinung mit Begründung (≈ 90) · Schluss (≈ 50).
4
Sprachliche Highlights einplanen: ein Konditionalsatz (If schools invested in …), ein Passiv (Students are often distracted by …), ein Relativsatz, zwei Verbindungswörter pro Absatz.
✓
Schreiben und prüfen: nach dem Schreiben 10 Minuten Korrektur – Inhaltspunkte abhaken, Wortanzahl, dann gezielt nach den eigenen typischen Fehlern suchen (siehe Fehlerfallen).

Stil im Writing

Keine Kurzformen im Essay und Report (do not statt don't). Keine Wiederholung derselben Verbindungswörter. very good lieber durch präzise Wörter ersetzen: excellent, outstanding, impressive. Und: Jeder Absatz braucht einen klaren ersten Satz, der sagt, worum es geht.

Task 2 · Learning to Be Bored6 Punkte

Read the article. For questions 1–6 choose the correct answer (A, B, C or D).
Learning to Be Bored

Most of us reach for our phones the moment we have nothing to do. Waiting for a bus, standing in a queue, even sitting at a red light – every empty minute seems to demand to be filled. Yet a growing number of psychologists argue that this habit may be costing us something valuable.

Dr Helen Marsh, who has studied boredom for over a decade, believes that moments of mental emptiness are when the brain does some of its most important work. 'When we are bored, the mind starts to wander,' she explains. 'It connects memories, makes plans for the future and plays with ideas. That is exactly the kind of thinking we need for creative problem-solving.'

In one of her experiments, two groups of students were asked to come up with unusual uses for a paper cup. Before the task, the first group spent fifteen minutes copying numbers from an old telephone directory – a deliberately dull activity – while the second group went straight to work. The students who had been bored produced significantly more original ideas.

Marsh is careful, however, not to romanticise boredom. 'Chronic boredom, the kind that lasts for weeks, is linked to low mood and risky behaviour,' she says. 'What I am talking about is short, everyday boredom that we no longer allow ourselves to experience.'

Parents, she suggests, should resist the urge to plan every minute of their children's free time. A child who complains that there is nothing to do is often just a few minutes away from inventing a game. For adults, her advice is simple: leave your phone in your pocket the next time you wait for a train, and see where your thoughts take you.

Not all researchers agree. Some point out that the experiments were small and that creativity is notoriously hard to measure. Marsh accepts the criticism. 'We need larger studies,' she admits. 'But the cost of trying is very low. The worst thing that can happen is that you look out of the window for five minutes.'

Task 4 · The Repair Café6 Punkte

Read the article and answer the questions in no more than four words.
Fix It Together

Every second Saturday, the community hall in Brookside turns into a workshop. Volunteers with screwdrivers, sewing machines and soldering irons sit at long tables, and residents queue up with broken toasters, torn jackets and bicycles with flat tyres. Welcome to the Brookside Repair Café.

The idea came from Tom Avery, a retired electrician who was tired of seeing perfectly good appliances thrown away. 'Most things break because of one small part,' he says. 'People just don't know how simple the repair can be.'

The café opened three years ago with four volunteers. Today there are twenty-two, including a former tailor, two engineering students and a twelve-year-old who specialises in game controllers. Repairs are free, but visitors are asked to leave a donation, which pays for spare parts and coffee.

What makes the café different from a repair shop is that visitors are expected to help. 'We don't just fix things for you,' explains Avery. 'We fix them with you. Next time, you might be able to do it yourself.'

Last year, the volunteers repaired more than 900 items, which, according to their own calculations, saved around four tonnes of waste. The most common problem? Loose connections in kettles.

Not every repair succeeds. About one in five items cannot be saved, usually because spare parts are no longer available. Avery hopes that new rules on the 'right to repair' will make it easier to get parts in future.

Protecting Teenagers Online: Is a Ban the Answer?

For most teenagers, social media is as much a part of daily life as school or sport. They use it to stay in touch with friends, follow their interests and express themselves. At the same time, concerns about its effects on young people are growing, and some governments are considering banning it for everyone under sixteen. But would such a ban really protect teenagers?

Supporters of a ban put forward several convincing arguments. Firstly, many parents worry that endless scrolling keeps their children awake at night and distracts them from homework. Secondly, platforms are designed to be addictive, which makes it extremely difficult for young people to control the time they spend online. Furthermore, teenagers are often exposed to cyberbullying and to unrealistic images of beauty and success, which can damage their self-esteem. From this point of view, a clear age limit seems like a simple and effective solution.

However, a ban would also have serious disadvantages. To begin with, it would be very hard to enforce, since tech-savvy teenagers could easily lie about their age or use their parents' accounts. What is more, social media is not only a source of risks. For many young people, especially those who feel lonely or different, online communities offer support and a sense of belonging. If these were taken away overnight, some teenagers might feel more isolated than before. Finally, a ban could simply postpone the problem: at sixteen, young people would suddenly be confronted with the online world without ever having learned how to handle it.

In my view, there are better ways to protect young people. Schools should teach media literacy from an early age, so that students learn to recognise manipulation, protect their privacy and deal with online conflicts. In addition, platforms should be required by law to offer safer settings for younger users, such as time limits and stricter controls on harmful content. Parents, too, have an important role to play: instead of simply forbidding apps, they could talk openly with their children about their experiences online.

All in all, I believe that a ban would send a strong signal but would fail to solve the real problem. Young people need guidance and better protection rather than a closed door. If we give them the skills to use social media responsibly, they will be far better prepared for the digital world they will grow up in.
